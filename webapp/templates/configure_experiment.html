Experiment for Financial Planning Advisor

The primary metric is your main success criterion for this experiment. This is the metric you'll use to decide if the treatment variant wins. Choose the metric that best represents your business goal. Sample size and power calculations are based on this metric.

The smallest relative improvement (in %) you want to be able to detect with your chosen power. This is your target effect size. Smaller effects require larger sample sizes. Example: Setting 5% means you want to detect at least a 5% relative improvement in your primary metric.

Alpha is your significance level — the threshold for deciding if your test result is statistically significant. It controls the risk of false positives (thinking there's a difference when there isn't). 👉 Most people use α = 0.05, meaning you require 95% confidence before declaring a winner.

Power is the probability of detecting a real difference when it actually exists. It controls the risk of false negatives (missing a real effect). Higher power = better chance of catching true improvements. 👉 Most people aim for 80% power, which is a good balance between cost and confidence.

Select additional metrics to track during the experiment. These will be monitored but won't be used for primary decision-making. The primary metric is automatically included.

How to split traffic between control and treatment. 50/50 is most common and statistically efficient. Unequal splits may be useful when treatment has higher costs or risks. Note: Unequal splits require larger total sample sizes.

p-value: "If there were really no difference between treatment and control, how surprising would this data be?" When the p-value is smaller than our threshold α (for example 0.05), we say the result is statistically significant and reject H0 the "no difference" hypothesis (aka null hypothesis).

95% confidence interval (aka CI): The 95% confidence interval for (treatment − control) is a range of effect sizes that are plausible given the data. If the entire interval is above 0, the treatment is likely better. If it is below 0, the treatment is likely worse. If it crosses 0, the data are compatible with "no real difference," and any true effect is likely small and somewhere inside that range.
